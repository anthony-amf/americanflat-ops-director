# Judgment, discretion, and empathy

The policy files tell a rep what the rule is. This file tells them how much room they
have around it, and how to sound like a person while using it.

**The organizing principle: be generous with warmth, deliberate with money.**

Warmth is free and always available — no rep needs permission to be kind, patient, or
genuinely apologetic. Money and freight cost real margin, so latitude there is
structured, bounded, and sometimes escalated. Reps who confuse the two either give away
margin to seem nice, or stay cold because they think rigidity is the safe choice. Both
are failures.

---

## This is Americanflat's own instruction, not an override

Discretion here is authorized by the SOPs, not invented against them:

> Use your judgement and try to keep Americanflat profitable when making a decision.
> When in doubt, ask help from senior management.
> — *Refund Policy, How To: Returns/Replacements/Refunds*

And the business case for bending is stated outright in the policy list of situations
where we offer a remedy:

> The customer is angry (to avoid negative reviews — it will be less expensive for the
> company to keep the customer happy than get a bad image).
> — *How To: Customer Service Policies, Replacements → Situations*

Read those together and the actual standard is clear: **the goal is the best commercial
outcome, not rule compliance.** A rep who follows the letter of policy into a 1-star
review, a chargeback, and a lost repeat customer has not done their job well.

So when this file and a literal reading of a policy line pull apart, the question is
never "what does the rule say" — it's "what does this rule exist to protect, and is it
protecting it here?"

---

## First: can it be bent at all?

Reps get this wrong constantly, in both directions. Two very different kinds of "no" get
treated as one:

| | **Capability limit** | **Policy choice** |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | Physically or operationally impossible | A business decision we made |
| **Can it bend?** | **No.** Never promise it | **Yes**, within the bounds below |
| **Examples** | Redirecting a package already shipped · cancelling an order already processing · order details for items sold *and shipped by* Amazon · holding stock we don't have | Restocking fee · who pays return shipping · replacement after day 30 · keep-it discount size · whether we need photos this time |

Bending a capability limit isn't generosity, it's a promise that will be broken — and
the second conversation is far worse than a clear first "we can't." Bending a policy
choice is often just good business.

**Say the difference out loud in the answer.** "We can't redirect it — that's not a
policy thing, the carrier already has it" lands completely differently from "our policy
doesn't allow that."

## The cost-of-remedy test

Before deciding how much room to take, weigh the remedy against what the alternative
actually costs. Not just the refund — the review, the churn, the rep time, the
escalation.

| Remedy cost | Posture | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Under ~$50** | **Just fix it.** Don't make them prove it, don't make them wait, don't ask a second time | Policy already says under $50 the customer keeps the item and you issue the remedy. A frame is worth less than the ticket handling it, let alone a bad review |
| **~$50–150** | **Use the ladder generously.** Replacement or store credit, offered warmly and immediately. Ask for photos, but frame it as "so we can get the right one out" not as verification | Real money, still cheaper than a churned customer. This is the band where warmth converts a complaint into loyalty |
| **Above ~$150, or repeat requests from one customer** | **Escalate before promising.** Holding reply, then your manager | Beyond a rep-level call, and pattern cases need a human who can see the pattern |

These bands are guidance for weighing the call, **not a spend authority**. See *Authority*
below — the actual approval limit is unset and needs Anthony's decision.

Judgment cuts both ways. A $12 poster does not need photo verification and a 3-day
round trip. A $400 order from a customer on their fourth claim is not a goodwill case
just because they're upset.

## Signals that argue for latitude

None of these decide it alone. Two or three together make a strong case.

**Lean generous when:**

- **It's our fault.** Damaged, defective, wrong item, lost by our carrier. Policy already
  says no return cost to them — don't add friction on top of our own error.
- **First contact from this customer.** No history of claims. The cheapest loyalty you
  will ever buy.
- **They're barely outside the line.** Day 32 of a 30-day window. $9 over a threshold.
  Enforcing a boundary to the day, against someone otherwise reasonable, reads as
  contempt and costs more than the gap.
- **There was a real occasion.** A gift, a wedding, a memorial, a birthday that has now
  passed. The product failed at something that mattered, and no refund fixes the timing.
  Acknowledge that specifically.
- **They're distressed rather than working an angle.** Long message, specific detail,
  apologetic about complaining — that's a person who wanted this to work.
- **They've already been let down twice.** A second failure on the same order is a
  different situation from a first. Stop optimizing the remedy and just make it right.
- **They asked for less than we'd have given.** Someone requesting a partial refund when
  policy would grant a full replacement should be told so.

**Hold firm when:**

- **There's a pattern.** Repeat claims, escalating amounts, several "never arrived" reports.
  Route it to a manager rather than deciding — you may not see the whole history.
- **The ask is leverage, not resolution.** Review threats used as pressure, demands
  well beyond the order value, refusal of every remedy but cash. Stay warm, stay firm,
  escalate.
- **The item is genuinely non-returnable.** Washed or used items, Final Sale. This is a
  condition problem, not a policy preference.
- **Granting it needs someone else's approval.** Wholesale pricing, post-30-day
  replacement, anything above your authority. Holding reply.
- **The gap is large, not marginal.** Six months past the window is not day 32.
- **You'd be setting a precedent you can't repeat.** If it only works because nobody
  else asks, it's not a decision — it's an exception that needs a manager.

## Authority — needs Anthony's decision ⚠

The SOPs do not state a rep-level spend limit. Until one is set, this skill will not
invent one, because a fabricated authority figure is worse than an escalation.

**Current guidance in the absence of a limit:** a rep may act unilaterally on remedies
that policy already prescribes for the situation (replacement, store credit, the
15–25% keep-it discount, partial refunds for missing accessories, keep-it under $50).
Anything **beyond** what policy prescribes — a waived restocking fee, a replacement past
day 30, a full refund where policy says store credit, a goodwill gesture with no policy
basis — is a **manager call**, regardless of amount.

Once a limit exists, it belongs here, and the escalation table in `escalation.md` should
point at it. Two numbers would resolve most of the daily friction:

1. The dollar value a rep can resolve without asking.
2. Whether a rep may waive the 20% restocking fee on their own, and under what conditions.

Until then, when the rep is about to exceed prescribed policy, say plainly: *"This is
past what policy already allows, so it needs your manager — here's a holding reply."*

## Log the exceptions

When policy gets bent, note what and why on the ticket, and record the money in the
usual place (CX Refunds tab for Shopify refunds, replacement log for replacements).

This isn't bureaucracy — it's the only way the SOPs improve. **If reps keep bending the
same rule for the same reason, the rule is wrong and should be changed.** A repeatedly
waived restocking fee, or a 30-day window everyone quietly treats as 45, is data about a
policy that no longer matches reality. Bring those to whoever owns the SOP.

The five contradictions in `sources.md` exist partly because nobody was writing this
down.

## Empathy — the craft

Policy decides *what* the customer gets. This decides whether they feel taken care of
while getting it. Same outcome, delivered two ways, produces a loyal customer or a
1-star review.

### Lead with the fix, not the rule

Nobody wants to hear about our policy. They want to know what happens now.

> ✗ "Our policy states that returns must be made within 30 days of delivery, and unfortunately your order falls outside this window."
>
> ✓ "I've had a look and I can get a replacement out to you — it'll ship in 3–4 business days. You're a little past our usual 30-day window, but given the frame arrived damaged, that's on us to sort out."

The second version delivers the same information and mentions the same window. It just
doesn't open by explaining why the customer is wrong.

### Never cite "policy" as the reason

"Policy" is not a reason, it's a deflection, and customers hear it as *"I could help you
but I won't."* Give the actual reason, in human terms.

> ✗ "Unfortunately our policy does not allow cancellations."
>
> ✓ "I wish I could stop it for you — once an order is in our system the warehouse has usually already picked it, and we can't pull it back out. What I can do is make the return completely painless once it lands."

### Acknowledge the specific thing

Generic apology reads as a form letter. Name what actually happened.

> ✗ "We apologize for the inconvenience caused."
>
> ✓ "Opening a puzzle to find pieces missing is genuinely annoying — especially if you'd set the evening aside for it."

Note the second one doesn't cost anything. Warmth is the free part.

### Apologize once, then act

One clear apology carries weight. Four in a row reads as insincere and slightly panicked,
and it makes Americanflat sound like it fails often. Say it once, properly, then move to
the fix.

### Name the emotion when it's obviously there

If someone is clearly upset, pretending otherwise is worse than addressing it.

> ✓ "You've every right to be frustrated — this is the second time we've got this order wrong, and that's not the experience we want you to have."

This does more to de-escalate than any remedy, and it's what turns an angry customer
into a retained one — which is exactly the outcome the SOP's own "customer is angry"
clause is aiming at.

### Yes, and — never a bare no

Lead with what you *can* do. If there's a genuine no, sandwich it: something you can do,
the honest limit, then the path forward.

> ✗ "We can't change the address once the order has shipped."
>
> ✓ "Let me see what we can do here. The package is already with the carrier, so we can't redirect it at this point — but if it comes back to us I'll get it straight back out to the correct address, and I'll keep an eye on it so you don't have to chase us."

### Offer choices

A customer who feels powerless calms down when handed control. Two real options beat one
imposed remedy, even when you'd have granted the better one anyway.

> ✓ "Two options, whichever suits you better: I can send a replacement out today, or refund you in full — no need to return anything either way."

### Don't make them ask twice, or prove too much

If policy lets you resolve it, resolve it in this message. Every round trip costs them
patience and costs us a ticket handling. And when you do need photos, frame it as
logistics, not suspicion:

> ✗ "We require photographic evidence before we can process any claim."
>
> ✓ "Could you send a quick photo? It helps me get the right replacement out and lets our product team catch whatever went wrong."

### Own it without naming names

"The warehouse messed up" or "our carrier lost it" makes Americanflat sound like it can't
run its own operation. Use "we."

> ✓ "We got this one wrong."

### Match their register

A two-line question gets a two-line answer. Someone who wrote three warm paragraphs
should not get a curt reply. Someone brisk and transactional doesn't want "Have an
amazing day" — they want the tracking number.

The templates in `reply-templates.md` are the house voice, not a script. Cut or reorder
them to fit the person.

### Close the loop when you said you would

The single most common CX failure isn't a wrong decision, it's silence after a promise.
If you said you'd come back by Thursday, come back by Thursday — even if the answer is
"still working on it." An on-time non-answer beats a late resolution.

## Where empathy must not go

- **Never soften a capability limit into a maybe.** Kindness that implies we might
  redirect a shipped package creates a broken promise.
- **Never invent authority you don't have.** "I'll get that refunded for you" before a
  manager approves it is a commitment Americanflat then has to honour or retract.
- **Never compress a timeline to sound helpful.** 3–4 days processing and 5–7 days
  delivery are the real numbers. "Should be there in a couple of days" is a second
  complaint you're scheduling.
- **Never engage a review-for-product request**, however politely it's worded. Policy is
  no response. Warmth doesn't apply to a shakedown.
- **Never let empathy override a pattern.** The fourth claim from one account is a
  manager's call, no matter how sympathetic the message. Being kind to a bad actor is
  unkind to the business, and the SOP's instruction is to keep Americanflat profitable.
